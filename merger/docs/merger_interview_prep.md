# Merger Model Interview Prep

## The mechanical ones

**"Walk me through accretion/dilution."**
Combine both companies' earnings, adjust for every effect the deal itself introduces: synergies, incremental interest on new debt, foregone interest on cash used, incremental amortisation from the purchase price allocation, tax-effect the result, then divide by the pro forma share count. Compare that pro forma EPS to what the acquirer would have earned standing alone. If pro forma EPS is lower, the deal is dilutive; if higher, accretive.

**"Why is a cash deal more dilutive than a stock deal here?"**
A cash deal funded by debt costs the acquirer interest expense, which comes straight off pre-tax profit. A stock deal costs the acquirer share count instead: no interest drag, but more shares dividing up the same profit. Whichever costs less per rupee of purchase price wins on an EPS basis; here, new debt priced at 9.5% is cheaper than IFB's own ~36x earnings yield being diluted across new stock, but not cheap enough to avoid dilution either way; the grid on the Sensitivity tab shows exactly where each combination lands.

**"Why doesn't the incremental D&A create a tax benefit?"**
Because this is modelled as a share purchase, not an asset purchase. In a share deal, the acquirer inherits the target's existing tax basis in its assets; there's no step-up for tax purposes even though there is one for book purposes. The incremental book D&A from the purchase price allocation reduces reported profit but shields no actual cash tax. Asset deals work differently and would get a real tax benefit from the step-up, a distinction that matters for how a deal gets structured in practice.

**"Why is goodwill the residual and not directly calculated?"**
Goodwill is whatever's left over after you've allocated the purchase price to everything you can separately identify and value; for this simplified model, one amortisable intangible. A full purchase price allocation would also step up inventory, fixed assets, and recognise deferred tax liabilities on the step-ups; goodwill absorbs whatever premium remains unexplained by identifiable assets.

## The ones specific to this model

**"Is this a realistic deal?"**
More realistic than the LBO model in the same repo, but still illustrative. Havells' balance sheet strength (debt-free, large cash pile) and Lloyd's disclosed competitive weakness in white goods make a bolt-on acquisition in the appliance space a genuinely plausible strategic move. The specific target, premium, and financing terms here are constructed from public data to demonstrate the mechanics, not based on any actual deal.

**"Why is the deal dilutive if the strategic logic is sound?"**
Because Year 1 accretion/dilution measures accounting earnings per share, not strategic value. IFB trades at roughly 36x trailing earnings, a rich multiple for a thin-margin appliance business, and financing a chunk of that price with new debt costs more in Year 1 interest than IFB's earnings contribute. The breakeven synergy calculation shows what it would take to close that gap: roughly ₹992 Cr of run-rate synergies, nearly five times IFB's own EBIT. That's not achievable, which is the honest conclusion: the deal would need a multi-year payback horizon and a strategic rationale, not a same-year EPS justification.

**"What would make this deal actually work?"**
Either a materially lower purchase price, a higher stock component to reduce the debt-cost drag, or accepting near-term dilution in exchange for the strategic value of fixing Lloyd's scale problem, and being explicit that the payback is a multi-year story, not a Year 1 one.
